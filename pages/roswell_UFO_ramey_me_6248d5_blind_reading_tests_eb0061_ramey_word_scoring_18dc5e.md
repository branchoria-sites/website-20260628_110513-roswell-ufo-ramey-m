## Introduction

A fair test of disputed words in the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) should not begin with a polished transcript. It should begin with scoring specific locations on the document before anyone attempts to assemble a coherent message. This matters because the strongest Roswell-related claims often depend on a small number of highly contested words, such as phrases interpreted as “victims of the wreck”. Researchers studying the memo have repeatedly noted that expectation and prior beliefs can influence what readers think they see in ambiguous image data. A scoring system therefore needs to measure agreement at precise locations, distinguish between letter-level and word-level confidence, and compare independent readers before constructing any larger [narrative]({{ 'narrative/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering...One example of newly discovered evidence that apparently conflicts with...</span></span></span>

The key question is not whether a proposed transcript sounds plausible. The key question is whether multiple readers independently identify the same letters and words in the same places under controlled conditions.

## Why Location Comes Before Transcription

One recurring problem in [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo debates is that readers often encounter a completed transcript first and then evaluate individual words within that framework. Once a proposed sentence exists, every uncertain mark tends to be interpreted in a way that supports the surrounding text.

The [blind-reading]({{ 'blind-reading/' | relative_url }}) research conducted by James Houran and Kevin Randle showed that participants exposed to different story frames produced different [readings]({{ 'readings/' | relative_url }}) from the same image. Their findings suggest that ambiguous marks are especially vulnerable to suggestion effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering...One example of newly discovered evidence that apparently conflicts with...</span></span></span>

A fair scoring protocol therefore starts with locations rather than meanings:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Divide the visible memo into numbered reading zones.
2. Mark the exact area where a reader believes text exists.
3. Record the reading before showing any proposed transcript.
4. Lock that response before comparison with other readers.

</div>

This approach prevents a later transcript from influencing the original observation.

## Marking the Exact Reading Area

The most important implementation rule is that every claimed word must be tied to a specific region of the photograph.

Instead of asking, “Can you read the memo?”, a structured test asks:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Which line contains text?
* Where does the word begin?
* Where does it end?
* How many characters are visible?
* Which individual characters are identifiable?

</div>

A reader claiming to see “victims” should identify the same physical location as another reader making that claim. If two people report the same word but point to different image regions, that is not true agreement.

The University of Texas at Arlington's Roswell archive presents annotated transcriptions that map proposed readings onto specific portions of the memo. Regardless of whether one accepts those readings, the underlying principle is useful: claims become testable only when linked to exact positions on the image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.libraries.uta.edu/roswell/node/21" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.libraries.uta.edu">[UT Arlington Libraries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.libraries.uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Final Annotated Transcription (Lower &amp; Middle Sections). Location-based scoring also prevents selective quotation. A disputed word cannot be separated from the surrounding image evidence if its boundaries have been defined in advance.

## Separate Letters, Words and Confidence

Not all readings deserve the same evidential weight.

A fair system scores three distinct levels.

### Letter-Level Scores

Readers first identify individual letters.

For example:

PositionReader EntryConfidence1VHigh2IMedium3?Low

Letter scoring reveals where agreement genuinely exists. Even if readers disagree about a whole word, they may consistently identify certain characters.

### Word-Level Scores

Only after letter scoring should readers propose complete words.

For example:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* Reader A: VICTIMS
* Reader B: VISITORS
* Reader C: V?CTIMS

</div>

The degree of overlap can then be measured objectively.

This matters because some Ramey memo debates focus on complete words while overlooking uncertainty in the underlying letters. Claims about specific readings become much stronger when agreement exists at both levels.

### Confidence Scores

Each response should carry a confidence rating.

A simple scale might be:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* 1 = guess
* 2 = tentative
* 3 = moderate confidence
* 4 = strong confidence
* 5 = near certainty

</div>

A word identified by ten readers with low confidence should not automatically outweigh a word identified by six readers with high confidence. Recording confidence preserves information that would otherwise be lost.

## Compare Readers Before Building a Transcript

The most important safeguard is to compare independent results before attempting to reconstruct a sentence.

A fair procedure would proceed in stages:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Independent reading.
2. Submission of location, letters and confidence.
3. Aggregation of responses.
4. Measurement of agreement.
5. Transcript construction only after agreement analysis.

</div>

This reverses the order often seen in public debates, where a transcript is proposed first and evidence is gathered afterwards.

The [Houran–Randle study]({{ 'suggestion-study/' | relative_url }}) demonstrated how readily interpretation can be influenced by prior expectations. A scoring system that delays transcript construction helps reduce precisely that problem.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering...One example of newly discovered evidence that apparently conflicts with...</span></span></span>

## What Counts as Strong Agreement?

A disputed word should earn credibility through convergence rather than enthusiasm.

Strong agreement would typically require:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* Multiple readers selecting the same image region.
* Broad agreement on word length.
* Consistent identification of key letters.
* Similar confidence ratings.
* Results obtained before exposure to proposed transcripts.

</div>

By contrast, weak agreement occurs when readers arrive at the same word only after discussion, transcript sharing or knowledge of previous interpretations.

This distinction is particularly important for famous Ramey memo candidates such as “victims”. Supporters and critics alike have argued over specific readings for decades, but the evidential value depends less on the word itself than on whether independent readers can repeatedly identify it under controlled conditions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2015/10/digital-image-of-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogA Different Perspective: Digital Image of the Ramey Memo29 Oct 2015 — The only two viable proposed words here would be...</span></span></span>
