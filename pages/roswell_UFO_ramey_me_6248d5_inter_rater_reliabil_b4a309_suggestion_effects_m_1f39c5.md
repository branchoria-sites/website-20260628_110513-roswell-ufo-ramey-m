## Introduction

One of the most important findings in the debate over the Roswell [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) is that people did not simply disagree about individual letters. Their [readings]({{ 'readings/' | relative_url }}) changed depending on the story they were told before looking at the image. This is a classic example of a suggestion effect, sometimes called priming: expectations supplied in advance influence what observers believe they can see in an ambiguous stimulus. In the case of the Ramey memo, the question was not merely whether the text was difficult to read, but whether prior beliefs could become part of the reading itself.

The significance of this issue extends beyond any single disputed phrase. If readers consistently recover different words after being given different contextual cues, then the memo's most sensational interpretations become less persuasive. The image may still contain genuine text, but the confidence that a specific dramatic message is visible is weakened when expectation demonstrably alters what observers report seeing.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“aliens.” It is a UFO case that has reached such mythic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering...There is even a television series on the Warner-Brothers Network devote...</span></span></span>

## Can Expectations Make Words Appear?

The most direct examination of suggestion effects came from James Houran and Kevin Randle's study, *A Message in a Bottle*. Rather than debating individual decipherments, they tested whether context influenced interpretation. Participants were divided into different groups and given different explanations of what the document might contain before attempting to read it. One group received a Roswell-UFO framing, another was told the document related to atomic-bomb testing, and a third received no such thematic guidance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“aliens.” It is a UFO case that has reached such mythic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering...There is even a television series on the Warner-Brothers Network devote...</span></span></span>

This design addressed a fundamental problem in memo research. Many prominent readings had already been publicised before independent testing occurred. Readers approaching the image often knew that investigators claimed to see references to crashes, discs, wreckage, or bodies. The experiment therefore asked whether such expectations affected perception.

The answer was largely yes. The researchers found that participants frequently reported words and phrases that reflected the [narrative]({{ 'narrative/' | relative_url }}) they had been primed to expect. Rather than converging on a single stable transcription, interpretations shifted with the supplied story.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“aliens.” It is a UFO case that has reached such mythic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering...There is even a television series on the Warner-Brothers Network devote...</span></span></span>

## The UFO, Atomic-Bomb, and Blind Prompts

The three experimental conditions created a useful comparison.

In the UFO-oriented condition, participants approached the document expecting links to the Roswell controversy and possible concealment of unusual debris. Under those circumstances, readers were more likely to report language compatible with that narrative. Ambiguous marks could be interpreted as references to crashes, recovery operations, or other ideas already familiar from Roswell literature.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“aliens.” It is a UFO case that has reached such mythic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering...There is even a television series on the Warner-Brothers Network devote...</span></span></span>

In the atomic-bomb condition, participants received a very different framework. Instead of expecting a flying-saucer story, they were led toward military and nuclear-testing themes. Here, readers tended to produce interpretations more consistent with that context. The same blurred shapes that one group associated with a UFO narrative could generate substantially different readings when viewed through a nuclear-testing lens.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“aliens.” It is a UFO case that has reached such mythic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering...There is even a television series on the Warner-Brothers Network devote...</span></span></span>

The blind group provided the crucial comparison. These participants were not supplied with a dramatic explanatory story before examining the image. Their responses therefore offered a rough baseline for what observers could recover without narrative guidance. The fact that results differed across the three conditions indicated that interpretation was not driven solely by the visible marks on the page. Expectations contributed materially to the outcome.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“aliens.” It is a UFO case that has reached such mythic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering...There is even a television series on the Warner-Brothers Network devote...</span></span></span>

## Why Ambiguous Text Invites Expectation Effects

The [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo photograph is especially vulnerable to suggestion because it contains several features known to encourage expectation-driven perception.

First, the image is highly degraded. The memo occupies only a small area of the original photograph, and the text is blurred, low-contrast, and partly obscured. Readers are often attempting to identify words from fragments rather than from complete letterforms.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://philpapers.org/rec/RANAGT-3" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: philpapers.org">[PhilPapers]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">philpapers.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">RANAGT 3</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">A Grounded Theory Update on the Roswell UFO Incident.by K Randle · 2022 · Cited by 2 — Attempts to decipher text from a photogr...</span></span></span>

Second, language naturally encourages pattern completion. When people encounter partial words, they do not process each letter independently. Instead, the brain uses context to predict likely completions. This is usually helpful in everyday reading, but it becomes problematic when the available visual information is extremely weak. Under those circumstances, expectation can fill gaps that the image itself does not reliably resolve.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-title">“aliens.” It is a UFO case that has reached such mythic</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering...There is even a television series on the Warner-Brothers Network devote...</span></span></span>

Third, the Roswell story provides unusually strong narrative cues. Decades of books, documentaries, lectures, and online discussions have circulated candidate phrases such as "victims of the wreck" and references to unusual debris. Once readers know those proposed solutions, it becomes difficult to approach the image as a blank puzzle. ## A Concrete Example: The Problem with Sensational Phrases

Suggestion effects matter most when evaluating the memo's most controversial readings.

## How Priming Weakens Sensational Readings

The key lesson from the priming experiments is not that the memo contains no recoverable text. Rather, it is that ambiguity and expectation interact.

If a phrase survives changes in context, survives different enhancement methods, and survives independent examination, confidence in that phrase increases. If a phrase appears mainly when readers are encouraged to anticipate a particular story, confidence decreases.
