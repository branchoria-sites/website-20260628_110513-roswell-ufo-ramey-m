## Introduction

Among all the disputed attempts to read the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}), one short fragment has consistently stood apart from the more sensational claims: **“Fort Worth, Tex.”** Unlike proposed [readings]({{ 'readings/' | relative_url }}) such as “victims of the wreck” or references to a crashed disc, this location appears in multiple independent examinations and requires far fewer uncertain letters to identify. The fragment therefore occupies a special place in discussions of whether the memo can be read at all. Rather than demonstrating that the entire document is recoverable, it illustrates a more modest conclusion: isolated, high-contrast text may survive even when the surrounding message does not. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.libraries.uta.edu/roswell/node/21" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.libraries.uta.edu">[UTA Libraries+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.libraries.uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UTA LibrariesDeciphering the Ramey Memo &#124; Roswell UFO Incident18 Jan 2017 — “FORT WORTH, TEX”. This distinction is important because the strongest evidence for the memo's [legibility]({{ 'legibility/' | relative_url }}) comes not from complete transcriptions but from small, repeatable fragments. The apparent reading of “Fort Worth, Tex.” is widely regarded as the most defensible example of that limited success.

## Why short fragments are easier to defend

Reading a degraded historical document is fundamentally different from reading an intact page. Every uncertain letter multiplies the chance of error. A proposed ten-word sentence requires dozens of individual character identifications to be correct simultaneously, whereas a place name may require recognition of only a handful of distinctive letter shapes.

The [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo presents exactly this problem. The paper occupies only a small portion of the original photograph, is held at an angle, and suffers from blur and loss of detail. Under those conditions, analysts generally agree that isolated words with distinctive patterns are much more likely to be identified correctly than long continuous passages. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering...One example of newly discovered evidence that apparently conflicts with...</span></span></span>

Several factors make a location such as "Fort Worth, Tex." especially favourable:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **It is short.** Fewer letters mean fewer opportunities for cumulative error.
* **Its structure is distinctive.** "Fort", "Worth", and the abbreviation "Tex." have recognisable word lengths and spacing.
* **It fits the document's context.** The memo was being handled during events at Fort Worth Army Air Field, making the appearance of the location unsurprising rather than extraordinary.
* **It resembles ordinary military correspondence.** Routine military messages commonly included datelines or location references.

</div>

None of these factors proves the reading is correct, but together they make it substantially more plausible than dramatic reconstructions built from many uncertain characters.

## The repeated Fort Worth reading

One notable feature of the "Fort Worth, Tex." interpretation is that it has appeared repeatedly across different examinations rather than emerging from a single decoding effort.

The most explicit endorsement comes from the University of Texas at Arlington's Roswell archive, which discusses the history of attempts to decipher the memo. Its assessment describes **“FORT WORTH, TEX”** as having **very high confidence**, noting that the fragment appears independently legible in multiple scans and that the spelling "TEX" is consistent with 1940s military typing conventions rather than the later postal abbreviation "TX". <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://sites.libraries.uta.edu/roswell/node/21" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sites.libraries.uta.edu">[UTA Libraries]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sites.libraries.uta.edu</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UTA LibrariesDeciphering the Ramey Memo &#124; Roswell UFO Incident18 Jan 2017 — “FORT WORTH, TEX”. Confidence: Very High. Independently legib...</span></span></span>

This is significant because the archive is not presenting a complete transcription of the memo. Instead, it distinguishes between fragments that repeatedly emerge and larger readings that remain speculative.

The apparent location also makes historical sense. The famous press photographs were taken during the 8 July 1947 briefing at Fort Worth Army Air Field after debris from Roswell had been flown there for examination. A reference to Fort Worth within a military message from that setting would therefore be entirely ordinary rather than unexpected. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/rosswe1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WHS ESDReport of Air Force Research Regarding the &quot;Roswell...27 Jul 1994 — Air Force involvement in the alleged UFO-related incident pop...</span></span></span>

Importantly, the plausibility of the location does not depend on any extraordinary interpretation of the Roswell incident itself. Even someone who rejects all UFO-related claims could reasonably expect a military memo from that occasion to mention Fort Worth.

## Why this fragment carries more weight than dramatic phrases

The contrast between "Fort Worth, Tex." and more controversial alleged readings illustrates an important principle in document interpretation.

The location fragment is comparatively strong because:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* it contains a limited number of letters;
* it has been reported independently by multiple analysts rather than only one reconstruction;
* it is historically unsurprising;
* it does not require unusual wording or assumptions.

</div>

By contrast, longer claimed phrases such as references to crash victims or recovered discs depend on many more uncertain characters arranged into coherent sentences. Once enough ambiguous marks are interpreted together, confirmation bias becomes a serious concern: viewers may unconsciously complete missing letters into the message they already expect to find. This problem has been discussed explicitly in analyses of the memo's decipherment, which warn that degraded images encourage subjective pattern recognition. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering...One example of newly discovered evidence that apparently conflicts with...</span></span></span>

The practical consequence is that confidence does not increase smoothly with the number of words recovered. It often decreases.

## The gap between fragments and a full message

The existence of one relatively credible fragment should not be mistaken for evidence that the entire memo can be reconstructed.

This distinction has been reinforced by official and independent examinations alike. During the 1994 Air Force investigation into Roswell, one of the Ramey photographs was submitted for [digital enhancement]({{ 'digital-sleuthing/' | relative_url }}) by a national-level image-analysis organisation. The resulting assessment concluded that the available photographs lacked sufficient quality to reveal the desired textual detail. <span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.esd.whs.mil/Portals/54/Documents/FOID/Reading%20Room/UFOsandUAPs/rosswe1.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: esd.whs.mil">[WHS ESD]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">esd.whs.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">WHS ESDReport of Air Force Research Regarding the &quot;Roswell...27 Jul 1994 — Air Force involvement in the alleged UFO-related incident pop...</span></span></span>

That finding does not imply that every letter on the page is unreadable. Instead, it suggests a mixed picture:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a few isolated fragments may survive;
* much of the page remains below the threshold for reliable interpretation;
* image enhancement cannot recreate detail that was never recorded in the original photograph.

</div>

Consequently, the strongest evidence supports selective rather than comprehensive legibility.
