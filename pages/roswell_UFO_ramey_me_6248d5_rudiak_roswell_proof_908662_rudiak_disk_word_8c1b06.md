## Introduction

In David [Rudiak]({{ 'rudiak/' | relative_url }})’s reconstruction of the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}), few words carry more weight than **“disk”** (sometimes rendered as “disc”). The reason is straightforward: if that word genuinely appears in a private military message connected to the Roswell incident, it could suggest that senior officers were still referring to the recovered object as a flying disc even while the public explanation was shifting toward a weather balloon. Yet the importance of the word also creates a problem. Because the memo is blurred and partially obscured, the debate is not merely whether “disk” would be significant, but whether the document actually contains that word at all. The entire evidential value of the claim depends on a disputed reading of a tiny fragment of text.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufocasebook.com/rameymemo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufocasebook.com">[UFO Casebook]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufocasebook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO CasebookRoswell Smoking Gun, the Ramey MemoIn speaking of &quot;THE VICTIMS OF THE WRECK&quot;,, using the word &quot;DISC&quot; for the crash object, an...</span></span></span>

## Did the Memo Really Say Disk?

Rudiak treats “disk” as one of the key anchor points in his broader reconstruction of the memo. In his published interpretation, the word appears alongside other controversial phrases such as “the victims of the wreck”. He argues that the memo's language makes best sense if military personnel were discussing a crashed disc-shaped object rather than ordinary balloon equipment. According to his reasoning, “disk” is not an isolated fragment but part of a coherent [narrative]({{ 'narrative/' | relative_url }}) embedded within the document.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufocasebook.com/rameymemo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufocasebook.com">[UFO Casebook]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufocasebook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO CasebookRoswell Smoking Gun, the Ramey MemoIn speaking of &quot;THE VICTIMS OF THE WRECK&quot;,, using the word &quot;DISC&quot; for the crash object, an...</span></span></span>

The difficulty is that the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo has never been definitively deciphered. Researchers attempting to analyse the photograph have repeatedly noted the poor quality of the source image. The document is visible only in a press photograph, held at an angle, folded, and reproduced at relatively low resolution. Studies of the memo have emphasised that interpretation often depends on fitting ambiguous marks into expected words rather than reading clearly visible letters.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/profile/James-Houran/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case/links/0deec527833e585cd6000000/A-Message-in-a-Bottle-Confounds-in-Deciphering-the-Ramey-Memo-from-the-Roswell-UFO-Case.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate+2ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate&#x27;&#x27;A Message in a Bottle:&quot; Confounds in Deciphering...One example of newly discovered evidence that apparently conflicts with...</span></span></span>

As a result, the question is not simply whether “disk” would matter if present. The prior question is whether the available image data support that reading strongly enough to distinguish it from alternative possibilities.

## Where Disk Fits in the Reconstruction

Within Rudiak’s model, “disk” functions as a connecting term linking several disputed phrases together.

His argument can be summarised as follows:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* The memo allegedly refers to a “disk”.
* It allegedly mentions a “wreck”.
* It allegedly refers to “victims”.
* It allegedly discusses transport or handling of recovered material.

</div>

Taken together, these elements are presented as evidence that the memo describes a crash recovery operation rather than [balloon debris]({{ 'balloon-debris/' | relative_url }}). In that framework, “disk” serves as the memo's label for the recovered object itself. Remove the word, and the larger interpretation becomes considerably weaker.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufocasebook.com/rameymemo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufocasebook.com">[UFO Casebook]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufocasebook.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO CasebookRoswell Smoking Gun, the Ramey MemoIn speaking of &quot;THE VICTIMS OF THE WRECK&quot;,, using the word &quot;DISC&quot; for the crash object, an...</span></span></span>

This is why discussions of the memo often return to the same handful of words. Rudiak's case is cumulative, but “disk” is one of the central pieces that allows the cumulative argument to exist in the first place.

## Why a Single Word Changes the Document

A genuine reference to a “disk” would matter because it would potentially reveal a difference between internal and public language.

On 8 July 1947, the Roswell Army Air Field initially announced recovery of a “flying disc”, terminology that was already dominating newspaper coverage during the nationwide flying-saucer scare. The military quickly replaced that narrative with the weather-balloon explanation promoted by General Ramey's office. If the memo privately used the term “disk” after that shift had begun, supporters argue it would suggest that officials were discussing something different from the public account.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roswell_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roswell incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell incident</span></span></span>

Critics respond that this conclusion assumes too much. Even if the word were present, it would not automatically prove extraterrestrial hardware, a crash, or a cover-up. It would only demonstrate that somebody used a term already widespread in public discourse. The evidential leap from “disk” to “alien craft” is much larger than the leap from “disk” to “object associated with the flying-disc story”.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://kevinrandle.blogspot.com/2018/12/david-rudiaks-latest-on-ramey-memo.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: kevinrandle.blogspot.com">[Kevin Randle&#x27;s Blog]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">kevinrandle.blogspot.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">david rudiaks latest on ramey memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Kevin Randle&#x27;s BlogDavid Rudiak&#x27;s Latest on the Ramey Memo19 Dec 2018 — Just because the word “DISC” is in the teletype, assuming it real...</span></span></span>

This distinction is often overlooked. The word's significance depends not only on whether it can be read in the memo but also on what meaning should be attached to it.

## Alternative Reasons Disc Language Might Appear

One of the strongest criticisms of the “disk” argument is historical context.

By July 1947, “flying disc” and “flying saucer” had become standard vocabulary throughout the United States. Newspapers, military offices, wire services, and witnesses routinely used such terminology when discussing unusual aerial reports. The Roswell press release itself employed flying-disc language before the balloon explanation emerged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roswell_incident" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: Wikipedia">[Wikipedia]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">Wikipedia</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Roswell incident</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Roswell incident</span></span></span>

Because of this environment, sceptical researchers argue that a reference to a “disk” would not necessarily be surprising. Several alternative explanations exist:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* The memo could have been discussing the public flying-disc reports rather than describing the recovered material.
* The term might have been used informally as shorthand for the Roswell case itself.
* The word could have appeared in a summary of press coverage or public reactions.
* The apparent reading could be incorrect altogether, with image noise creating the illusion of familiar words.

</div>
