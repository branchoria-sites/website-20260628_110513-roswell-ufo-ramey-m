## Introduction

The short answer is yes: the wording used before people view the [Ramey memo]({{ 'ramey-memo/' | relative_url }}) can materially change what they believe they are reading. This matters because the memo is not a normally legible document. It is a small, blurred sheet of paper visible in a 1947 press photograph connected to the Roswell incident. When viewers are presented with an ambiguous image, their expectations can help determine which marks look like letters, which letters look like words, and which words seem to fit a story. Research on the memo found that readers given different contextual prompts often produced different interpretations of the same image, making prompt design a central issue in any [blind reading]({{ 'blind-reading/' | relative_url }}) test.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) “A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering...“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo f...</span></span></span>

The debate is therefore not only about what is on the paper. It is also about how human perception works when confronted with degraded visual evidence.

## What Readers Are Told Before Viewing

The key mechanism is expectation-driven perception. When an image contains incomplete or uncertain information, viewers do not simply record what is present. They unconsciously combine the visible marks with assumptions about what they expect to find.

In the best-known study of the Ramey memo, James Houran and Kevin Randle divided participants into three groups. One group was told the document related to the Roswell UFO case. A second group was told it concerned secret atomic-bomb testing. A third group received no content-related explanation before attempting to decipher the text. The researchers then [compared]({{ 'compared/' | relative_url }}) the resulting [readings]({{ 'readings/' | relative_url }}).<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) “A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering...“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo f...</span></span></span>

The important finding was not merely that people disagreed. Rather, many of the proposed readings reflected the [narrative]({{ 'narrative/' | relative_url }}) supplied beforehand. Participants often organised uncertain shapes into words that made sense within the story they had been given. The same visual stimulus generated different apparent messages depending on the framing context.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) “A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering...“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo f...</span></span></span>

This is a classic example of what psychologists sometimes call top-down processing. Instead of perception flowing only from image to interpretation, interpretation can also flow from expectation to image.

## Why Roswell and Atomic-Test Prompts Matter

The Roswell prompt and atomic-test prompt were deliberately chosen because each offered a coherent but very different narrative framework.

A participant who hears that a document is connected to Roswell may begin looking for military references, crash descriptions, unusual objects, recovery operations or other terms associated with long-running UFO discussions. A participant told the document concerns atomic testing may instead look for references to weapons, experiments, [test ranges]({{ 'test-range/' | relative_url }}), security procedures or military reporting language.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) “A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering...“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo f...</span></span></span>

The critical point is that neither participant necessarily intends to distort the evidence. The effect is often unconscious. Ambiguous shapes can plausibly support several competing letter combinations. Once a reader begins favouring one interpretation, neighbouring characters may also start to fit the same narrative.

This is why disputed phrases in the memo have remained controversial. Supporters of different interpretations frequently point to words they consider obvious, while opponents argue that those words emerge only after a reader already knows what they are supposed to see. The Houran-Randle experiment was designed specifically to test that concern.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) “A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering...“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo f...</span></span></span>

The findings do not prove that every proposed memo reading is incorrect. They do show that context can be powerful enough to steer readers toward different solutions.

## How a Neutral Prompt Changes the Process

A neutral prompt attempts to remove narrative guidance before the first viewing. Instead of mentioning Roswell, UFOs, weather balloons, atomic tests or military secrets, participants are simply shown the image and asked to identify any visible text.

This changes the task in several ways.

**It reduces narrative anchoring.** Readers begin with fewer assumptions about what kinds of words should appear.

**It exposes genuine agreement.** If multiple independent viewers identify the same word without being steered toward a theme, that agreement becomes more informative.

**It separates perception from interpretation.** Readers can first report what letters or words they think they see, then discuss possible meanings afterwards.

**It makes disagreement easier to measure.** Researchers can compare where readers independently converge and where they diverge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/228706129_A_Message_in_a_Bottle_Confounds_in_Deciphering_the_Ramey_Memo_from_the_Roswell_UFO_Case" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: researchgate.net">[ResearchGate]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">researchgate.net</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ResearchGate(PDF) “A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering...“A Message in a Bottle:” Confounds in Deciphering the Ramey Memo f...</span></span></span>

The result is not necessarily a final decipherment. Instead, it provides a cleaner picture of which parts of the image are producing consistent observations and which parts are being shaped by expectation.

## How a Cleaner First-Round Prompt Would Work

A stronger blind-reading design for the [Ramey]({{ 'ramey/' | relative_url }}) memo would make the first viewing as neutral as possible.

A simple approach would be:

<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--step-flow" markdown="1">

1. Present the image without mentioning Roswell or any alternative explanation.
2. Ask participants to mark only letters, words or short phrases they believe are visible.
3. Record confidence levels for each reading.
4. Compare results across independent readers before discussing the image's historical context.
5. Introduce competing interpretations only after the initial readings are complete.

</div>

Researchers studying ambiguous visual material often prefer this order because it allows spontaneous agreement to emerge before any explanatory framework is introduced.
